Attention Is All You Need

Scaled Dot-Product Attention: Queries, Keys, Values, Scaling, and Row-Wise Softmax

This lab lets you run the scaled dot-product attention computation yourself on a tiny teaching example: one query, three keys, and three values, each of length two. You can edit the query, the keys, the values, and an optional temperature extension, then watch the raw scores, the scaled scores, the row-wise softmax weights, and the final weighted blend update together. The goal is to build intuition for the four steps of the equation and for why the scaling factor matters, not to reproduce any reported experimental result.

Focus: Explain only scaled dot-product attention: the different roles of queries, keys and values, the scaling by sqrt(d_k), and row-wise softmax. Include the prerequisites and context needed for this equation, but do not explain the full Transformer architecture, its training recipe, or benchmark results.

Audience: Engineering undergraduate comfortable with basic algebra and vectors, new to attention.

What you will learn

  • understand Explain the distinct roles of queries, keys, and values in an attention function, and why the output is a weighted sum of values rather than of keys.
  • analyze Analyze why dot products of dimension d_k grow in magnitude as d_k increases, and explain how dividing by sqrt(d_k) counteracts the resulting softmax saturation and small gradients.
  • apply Apply the scaled dot-product attention equation to a small set of query, key, and value vectors, computing scores, applying row-wise softmax, and forming the weighted-sum output.
  • understand Compare scaled dot-product attention with additive attention and with unscaled dot-product attention, stating the practical and numerical reasons the paper gives for its choice.
01 / prerequisites

Prerequisites: Vectors, Dot Products, and Softmax (Added Background)

Before reading the attention equation, you need three ideas. A vector is just an ordered list of numbers. A dot product multiplies two vectors element by element and adds the results, giving one number that says how similar their directions are. Softmax takes a list of numbers and turns it into a set of positive weights that add up to one, so bigger numbers get bigger weights. Attention uses the dot product to score similarity and softmax to convert those scores into weights.

In more detail

This section is added teaching background, not content of the paper. The paper assumes the reader is comfortable with vectors and matrix products. Scaled dot-product attention operates on vectors: a query vector is compared with key vectors using a dot product, and the resulting numbers are turned into weights by a softmax. A dot product of two vectors of length d_k is the sum of the elementwise products, so it is a single scalar that measures alignment: it is large and positive when the vectors point in similar directions, near zero when they are unrelated, and negative when they oppose. Softmax converts a list of real numbers into a probability distribution: each output is positive, and the outputs sum to one, with larger inputs receiving larger shares. These two operations are the entire mathematical toolkit needed for the attention equation.

Build an intuition analogy

Think of a dot product as a similarity meter: two arrows pointing the same way give a high reading, perpendicular arrows give zero, and opposite arrows give a negative reading. Softmax is then a fair-share allocator that hands out 100 percent of a budget in proportion to those readings. The analogy stops at the fact that softmax is exponential rather than linear in its inputs, so it exaggerates differences rather than sharing proportionally.

Connect this idea

The attention equation itself · These prerequisites are exactly the operations that the attention equation composes: a matrix of dot products, a scaling, a softmax, and a weighted sum.

Where this applies

simplification This section is teaching background supplied for an audience new to attention; the paper itself does not define vectors, dot products, or softmax.

02 / problem

The Problem Attention Solves: Relating Positions Without Recurrence

Older sequence models read a sentence one step at a time, so step two cannot start until step one finishes. That makes training slow and makes it hard for information to travel between words that are far apart. Attention solves both problems: it lets each position look directly at every other position in one shot, so the connection between two distant words costs the same as the connection between two neighbors. Scaled dot-product attention is the specific recipe the paper uses to decide how much each position should look at each other position.

In more detail

The paper motivates attention as a way to model dependencies between positions in a sequence without regard to their distance in the input or output sequence (s0008). Recurrent models instead factor computation along symbol positions, generating hidden state h_t as a function of the previous hidden state h_{t-1} and the input at position t, and this inherently sequential nature precludes parallelization within training examples (s0007). Convolutional alternatives relate two positions with a number of operations that grows with the distance between them, linearly for ConvS2S and logarithmically for ByteNet, which makes learning dependencies between distant positions harder; in the Transformer this is reduced to a constant number of operations (s0011). Attention is therefore the mechanism that lets every position draw information from every other position directly, and the paper's self-attention variant relates different positions of a single sequence to compute a representation of that sequence (s0012).

Build an intuition analogy

A recurrent model is like passing a note down a long line of people: the message degrades and the line moves slowly. Attention is like everyone in the room hearing everyone else at once. The analogy stops at the fact that attention does not transmit a single shared message; each position builds its own private summary from the positions it chooses to weight.

Connect this idea

Why self-attention is used at all · The paper's later comparison of layer types quantifies these motivations with per-layer complexity, sequential operations, and maximum path length, but that comparison is outside this focused explanation.

Where this applies

limitation The paper notes that reducing the path length to a constant comes at the cost of reduced effective resolution due to averaging attention-weighted positions, an effect it counteracts with multi-head attention (s0011). That remedy is outside this focused explanation.

03 / method

The Attention Function: Query, Keys, Values, and Output

Attention has three inputs with three different jobs. A query is the thing asking a question: for one position, what information do I need? Keys are the labels that each candidate position offers for matching: how relevant am I to that question? Values are the actual content each candidate position would contribute if selected. The mechanism scores the query against every key, turns those scores into weights, and then returns a weighted average of the values. So keys decide who gets attention, and values decide what attention delivers.

In more detail

The paper defines an attention function as a mapping from a query and a set of key-value pairs to an output, where the query, keys, values, and output are all vectors (s0025). The output is computed as a weighted sum of the values, and the weight assigned to each value is computed by a compatibility function of the query with the corresponding key (s0025). This separation of duties is the core of the mechanism: keys are what a query is compared against to decide relevance, while values are what actually gets mixed into the output once relevance is known. The paper's particular instantiation is called Scaled Dot-Product Attention, whose input consists of queries and keys of dimension d_k and values of dimension d_v (s0027). Note that the key dimension and the value dimension are named separately, so the compatibility computation and the content being aggregated need not have the same width.

Build an intuition analogy

A library search is a close fit: your search phrase is the query, each book's title and index terms are its key, and the book's contents are its value. You match against titles, but you read the contents. The analogy stops at the fact that attention does not retrieve one book; it blends all of them in proportion to their match scores.

Connect this idea

Self-attention as a special case · When queries, keys, and values all come from the same source, the paper calls the mechanism self-attention or intra-attention; the roles stay distinct even though the source is shared.

Where this applies

assumption The definition assumes each query is paired with a set of key-value pairs; the paper does not specify in this section how those vectors are produced, which is a separate design choice handled by the surrounding architecture.

04 / method

The Scaled Dot-Product Attention Equation

The equation does four things in order. First, multiply Q by the transpose of K, which compares every query against every key and produces a grid of similarity scores. Second, divide every score by the square root of the key dimension, which keeps the numbers in a workable range. Third, apply softmax to each row of that grid, turning each row into weights that are positive and sum to one. Fourth, multiply those weights by V, so each output row is a weighted blend of the value vectors. The result has one output vector per query.

In more detail

The paper computes the attention function on a set of queries simultaneously, packed together into a matrix Q, with keys and values packed into matrices K and V, and gives the matrix of outputs as Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax(QK^T / sqrt(d_k)) V (s0028, s0029). Reading the expression left to right: QK^T produces a matrix of dot products between every query and every key; dividing by sqrt(d_k) scales those scores; softmax is applied to obtain the weights on the values; and multiplying by V forms the weighted sum. The paper states that the dot products of the query with all keys are divided by sqrt(d_k) and a softmax function is applied to obtain the weights on the values (s0027). Figure 2's left panel shows this pipeline as a block diagram: Q and K enter a matrix multiplication, the result passes through a Scale block, an optional Mask block, a SoftMax block, and finally a matrix multiplication with V (v0002).

Build an intuition analogy

Picture a scoreboard with one row per query and one column per key. The first matrix product fills in the scoreboard, scaling shrinks the entries, softmax converts each row into a set of percentages, and the final product uses those percentages to mix the value vectors. The analogy stops at the fact that the percentages are not a budget being spent; they are coefficients in a weighted average, so an output can lie anywhere in the span of the value vectors.

  1. Multiply the query matrix by the transpose of the key matrix to obtain the raw compatibility scores between every query and every key.
  2. Divide every raw score by the square root of the key dimension d_k, producing scaled scores whose magnitude does not grow with d_k.
  3. Apply softmax across each row of the scaled score matrix so that each query's weights over the keys are nonnegative and sum to one.
  4. Multiply the attention weights by the value matrix so that each query receives a weighted sum of the value rows.

Q and K must share the same second dimension d_k for QK^T to be defined; the resulting score matrix has shape (n_q, n_k).

The number of columns of the attention weight matrix must equal the number of rows of V, which holds because K and V are packed from the same set of key-value pairs.

Softmax is applied along the key axis, so each row of the weight matrix sums to 1; the output dimension d_v is independent of d_k.

The scaling factor is 1/sqrt(d_k), so d_k must be positive; the paper's variance argument assumes query and key components are independent with mean 0 and variance 1.

assumption The matrix form assumes all queries share the same dimension d_k and all values share dimension d_v, so that QK^T and the subsequent product with V are dimensionally compatible.

limitation The paper's equation as extracted does not state the softmax normalization axis explicitly; the row-wise reading used here follows from the requirement that each query's weights over the keys form a distribution, and from the paper's statement that softmax is applied to obtain the weights on the values.

05 / method

Why Divide by sqrt(d_k): Score Growth and Softmax Saturation

When you add up d_k products of random numbers, the total tends to wander further from zero as d_k grows: its variance is d_k, so its typical size grows like the square root of d_k. Large scores are a problem for softmax because softmax is exponential. If one score is much larger than the others, softmax puts nearly all the weight on that one entry and almost none on the rest, and in that regime its gradients become extremely small, so learning stalls. Dividing every score by sqrt(d_k) cancels the growth: the scaled scores keep roughly the same spread no matter how large the key dimension is. The paper keeps the dot-product form rather than switching to additive attention because dot products map onto fast matrix multiplication routines.

In more detail

The paper explains the scaling factor with a variance argument. If the components of q and k are independent random variables with mean 0 and variance 1, then their dot product q·k = sum over i of q_i k_i has mean 0 and variance d_k (s0031). So for large d_k the dot products grow large in magnitude, which pushes the softmax function into regions where it has extremely small gradients (s0031). The paper reports that for small values of d_k the additive and dot-product mechanisms perform similarly, but that additive attention outperforms unscaled dot-product attention for larger values of d_k (s0031). Dividing by sqrt(d_k) counteracts this effect, restoring the scores to a scale that does not grow with the dimension (s0031). The paper also notes that dot-product attention is much faster and more space-efficient in practice than additive attention, since it can be implemented using highly optimized matrix multiplication code, even though the two are similar in theoretical complexity (s0030).

Build an intuition analogy

Imagine averaging many coin flips: individual flips cancel out, but the running total still drifts further from zero the longer you go. Dividing by the square root of the number of flips keeps the drift at a fixed scale. The analogy stops at the fact that the dot product is a sum of products of two random quantities rather than a sum of independent flips, though the variance calculation works out the same way under the paper's independence assumption.

limitation The paper states its suspicion about large dot products pushing softmax into small-gradient regions as a suspicion, and cites prior work for the empirical comparison between additive and unscaled dot-product attention; it does not present a controlled experiment isolating the scaling factor in this section.

06 / method

Row-Wise Softmax and the Weighted Sum of Values

Softmax is applied to each row of the scaled score matrix separately. One row corresponds to one query, and its entries correspond to that query's scores against all the keys. After softmax, that row is a set of positive weights summing to one, so it reads as a percentage split of attention across the keys. Multiplying by V then produces, for that query, a blend of the value vectors in those proportions. Because the rows are normalized independently, each query gets its own distribution; queries do not compete with each other for weight. The optional mask step, shown in the figure, sets some scores to negative infinity before softmax so those positions receive essentially zero weight.

In more detail

After scaling, the paper applies a softmax function to obtain the weights on the values (s0027). Because the score matrix has one row per query and one column per key, applying softmax along the key axis makes each row a distribution over the keys for that query. The output is then the product of these weights with V, which realizes the paper's definition of attention as a weighted sum of the values, where the weight assigned to each value is computed by a compatibility function of the query with the corresponding key (s0025). The paper also shows that this softmax input can be modified before normalization: in the decoder's self-attention, it masks out illegal connections by setting the corresponding softmax inputs to negative infinity (s0045). Figure 2's left panel places an optional Mask block between Scale and SoftMax, consistent with that description (v0002).

Build an intuition analogy

Think of each query as holding a fixed pot of attention to distribute among the keys. Softmax decides the split of that pot based on the scaled scores, and the value vectors are then mixed in those proportions. The analogy stops at the fact that the pot is not a conserved resource across queries: every query has its own full pot, and the weights are coefficients in an average rather than quantities being transferred.

misconception A common error is to normalize over all scores at once rather than per row. Because each query must receive its own distribution over the keys, softmax is applied along the key axis, and each row sums to one independently.

limitation The paper describes masking as setting softmax inputs to negative infinity but does not specify the numerical convention used to represent negative infinity in practice; the extracted text does not resolve this implementation detail.

07 / conclusion

Takeaway and Scope of This Explanation

The whole mechanism fits in one sentence: score each key against the query with a scaled dot product, turn the scores into a probability distribution with softmax, and use that distribution to average the values. Everything else in the paper builds on this function, but understanding this function does not require understanding the rest of the architecture. If you can compute the four steps by hand for a handful of vectors, you understand scaled dot-product attention.

In more detail

Scaled dot-product attention, as the paper presents it, is a single function: compare a query against a set of keys by dot product, divide the scores by sqrt(d_k) to keep them at a stable scale, normalize each query's scores into weights with softmax, and return the weighted sum of the values (s0025, s0027, s0029, s0031). The paper positions this function as the building block of the Transformer, which it describes as relying entirely on attention mechanisms and dispensing with recurrence and convolutions (s0004, s0009). This explanation deliberately stops at the attention function itself. The paper's surrounding architecture, its multi-head extension, its training recipe, and its translation and parsing results are outside the scope covered here.

Connect this idea

Where this applies
